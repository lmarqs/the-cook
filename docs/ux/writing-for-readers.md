# Writing for readers

A page that sells Cook competes for a reader who scans, decides in a few lines whether the page is for them, and
leaves at the first exaggeration. A page built as kicker, slogan, lede and a grid of boxes gives that reader a
catalogue of what the product does and no reason to keep reading: it has no narrator, no progression, and it sells
mechanism as if it were benefit. This page states what the primary sources on writing and on web reading say about
writing from the reader's need, keeping one voice, ordering a page as problem, change and evidence, choosing the
concrete over the mechanism, and writing for how people read on screens, then gives the checklist a rewrite is tested
against. The voice the site keeps is the [philosophy](../philosophy.md)'s; the words are the
[glossary](../features/glossary.md)'s; the site's own rules are in [messages and docs](messages-and-docs.md).

## The stance: the reader needs to read something, and the writer knows what

Thomas and Turner call the stance classic style: "the writer, on the motive of truth, wanting nothing from the reader,
presents something", and "writer and reader are intellectual and aesthetic equals"
([Prose Style Writing Guide](https://classicprose.com/csguide.html)). The writer "knows something before he sets out
to write", and "truth needs no argument but only accurate presentation"
([excerpt](https://classicprose.com/csx.html)). The prose is "a perfect and perfectly transparent window on that
reality", and "we are not reading for the style but rather for what he presents"
([guide](https://classicprose.com/csguide.html)). Three consequences for a product page:

- The page shows the reader a situation they recognise and what changes in it. It does not plead, and it does not
  perform. A kicker that announces a section and a slogan that restates it are the writer drawing attention to the
  prose, which the stance rules out.
- The page has one narrator who has seen the thing and points at it. "The symmetry between writer and reader allows
  the presentation to follow the model of conversation" ([excerpt](https://classicprose.com/csx.html)); Zinsser says
  the same in his own terms, "Writing is talking to someone else on paper or on a screen"
  ([Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/)).
- What the reader needs to read is decided before a word is written. Basecamp's Getting Real puts the product's
  purpose, its vision, before any design or code, and keeps it short so every later decision can be checked against
  it ([What's the Big Idea?](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/04.1-whats-the-big-idea)). For Cook that sentence is the
  philosophy's: a configuration manager for repositories, with a reviewed diff
  ([philosophy](../philosophy.md#what-cook-is)).

## Write from the reader's need, not the product's features

Christensen's jobs theory gives the test. "The fact that you're 18 to 35 years old with a college degree does not
cause you to buy a product", and "the causal mechanism behind a purchase is, 'Oh, I've got a job to be done.'"
Looking at a product by its function "really originates from your competitors or your own employees deciding what
you need", whereas the jobs view "causes you to crawl into the skin of your customer and go with her as she goes
about her day" ([Clay Christensen's Milkshake Marketing, HBS Working
Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing)). The milkshake customers "faced a long,
boring commute and needed something to keep that extra hand busy"; the product was hired for that circumstance, not
for its thickness. The 2016 HBR statement of the theory opens on the same point, that firms "have never known more
about their customers, but their innovation processes remain hit-or-miss"
([Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done)).

Why it matters for copy: a feature grid is the function view. A sentence that opens with the reader's circumstance,
such as a second repository that needs the setup the first one has, is the job view, and the feature arrives as the
thing hired for it. Basecamp's promo site chapter orders the page that way: the overview should "Explain your app and
its benefits", the manifesto "Explain the philosophy and ideas behind it", and case studies "Provide real life
examples that show what's possible" ([A Powerful Promo Site](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/13.2-a-powerful-promo-site)).
Nielsen's 1997 study found users "detested 'marketese'", because "promotional language imposes a cognitive burden"
and "Credibility suffers when users clearly see that the site exaggerates"; rewriting a promotional page in objective
language alone measured 27% better in usability, and concise, scannable and objective together 124% better
([How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)).

Zinsser's reader is the reason the circumstance has to be a person's: "As readers, we want to be able to picture
specific people like ourselves", and abstract nouns in -ion "hide the people doing things"
([Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/)).

## Keep one narrator and one voice

Basecamp: "Copywriting is interface design", "every letter matters", and "You need to speak the same language as your
audience too" ([Copywriting is Interface Design](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/09.7-copywriting-is-interface-design)).
A page whose hero speaks as the lab, whose middle speaks as a feature list and whose end speaks as a reference manual
has three narrators and no audience.

Zinsser's fourth principle is humanity: "Your product, finally, is you", and the writer should write only what they
would comfortably say aloud ([Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/)).
Orwell's scrupulous writer asks of every sentence "What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or
idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?", and his first rule is "Never use a
metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print", because dying metaphors "are merely
used because they save people the trouble of inventing phrases for themselves"
([Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)).
The lab image passes that test only while it stays one image with one translation per term, as the
[philosophy](../philosophy.md#in-the-labs-words) fixes it; a second metaphor per section is prose "tacked together
like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house" (same essay).

Strunk gives the unit the voice is kept in: "Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic",
whose object "is, of course, to aid the reader" ([The Elements of Style, 1918](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt)).
A box in a grid is a paragraph with no neighbour, so nothing carries the reader from one to the next.

## Order the page as problem, change, evidence

Basecamp's rule for explaining anything new: "write a brief story about it", "Don't get into the technical or design
details, just tell a quick story", "Do it in a human way, like you would in normal conversation", and "Just give the
flow of what happens" ([Tell Me a Quick Story](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/11.3-tell-me-a-quick-story)). A story
has an order; a grid has none.

The web constrains the order. The inverted pyramid puts "the most important information (or what might even be
considered the conclusion)" first, so a reader who stops at any point still has the main point, and the rule applies
inside each element: "Frontload all elements of content with important information", open each paragraph with its key
sentence ([Inverted Pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)). The F-shaped pattern means "First
lines of text on a page receive more gazes than subsequent lines", and "First few words on the left of each line of
text receive more fixations than subsequent words"; the pattern "is bad for users and businesses", and good formatting
reduces it: the most important points in the first two paragraphs, headings that start with "the words carrying most
information" ([F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)).

So the sequence is: the problem stated as the reader's circumstance, in the first lines; what changes for them, as the
conclusion, before the mechanism; then the evidence, which for Cook is the page that backs each claim, as the
philosophy's tables already pair them ([philosophy](../philosophy.md#what-they-get)). Strunk's rule for the sentence
holds for the page: "Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end", because of "the prominence which it gives to
the main statement" ([Elements of Style](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt)); the ending of a
section is where the change for the reader lands, not where a link to the docs is dropped.

## Prefer the concrete thing to the mechanism

Strunk: "Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract", and "Make
definite assertions", using not "never as a means of evasion"
([Elements of Style](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt)). Orwell: "let the meaning choose the
word, and not the other way about"; "When you think of a concrete object, you think wordlessly"; pretentious diction
is "used to dress up simple statements"
([Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)).
Zinsser: Latin-derived words "will strangle and suffocate everything you write", Anglo-Saxon ones "will set you free",
and "One thought per sentence"
([Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/)).

Mechanism is abstract to a reader who has not used the tool. "Renders the policy into each harness's own files" is how
Cook works; "allow `git diff`, deny `git reset --hard`, once, and every harness obeys" is what the reader sees happen.
The diff on the page is the most concrete sentence on it, which is why it carries more than any box around it. The
mechanism belongs after the change, as its proof, and in the words the glossary fixes.

Positive form has a second reason on a selling page: a claim about what the alternative lacks reads as marketese
(Nielsen, above), while a claim about what the reader gets is checkable against the page that backs it.

## Write for how people read on a screen

Nielsen, 1997: people "rarely read Web pages word by word; instead, they scan the page"; 79% of test users "always
scanned any new page they came across; only 16 percent read word-by-word". The guidelines that followed: highlighted
keywords, "meaningful sub-headings" rather than clever ones, bulleted lists, "one idea per paragraph", the conclusion
first, and "half the word count (or less)"; concise text alone measured 58% better, scannable layout 47%
([How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)). In 2015: "On the average
web-page visit, users read only 28% of the words"; legibility, readability and comprehension are "necessary, but not
sufficient", and the page also needs a focus on the reader's interests and an immediate signal of value at the top; a
rewrite in user-centric language raised recall of product characteristics from 33% to 65%
([Legibility, Readability, and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/legibility-readability-comprehension/)).

Two things follow that a narrative page must reconcile. A story needs continuity, and a scanner reads the first line
of each block and the first words of each line. The resolution the sources give is that every block opens with its
point, in the reader's terms, so that the scanner reads the story's spine and the reader who stays reads the story.
Headings are the spine: a heading that names the reader's situation ("The second repository") carries information; a
heading that names the section's role ("The shift") carries none, and under the F-pattern it is one of the few things
read.

## Checklist

A page meant to sell Cook passes when every item holds. Each cites the source that makes it a rule.

1. The first two paragraphs state the reader's circumstance and what changes for them; a reader who stops there has
   the point ([Inverted Pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/),
   [F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)).
2. Each section opens with a job the reader has, in a situation they would recognise as their own day, and the
   feature arrives as what they hire for it; no section opens with a feature
   ([Milkshake Marketing](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing),
   [A Powerful Promo Site](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/13.2-a-powerful-promo-site)).
3. Sections run in one order, problem, change, evidence, and each hands to the next; no section stands as a grid of
   peers ([Tell Me a Quick Story](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal/11.3-tell-me-a-quick-story),
   [Elements of Style, rule 8](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt)).
4. One narrator, who has seen the thing and shows it, speaks on every section in the register of the philosophy;
   read aloud, every sentence is one the writer would say to a colleague
   ([classicprose.com guide](https://classicprose.com/csguide.html),
   [Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/)).
5. Every heading starts with the words that carry its information and names the reader's situation or the change,
   never the section's role ([F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/),
   [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)).
6. The change for the reader comes before the mechanism; the mechanism appears as the proof of the change, in
   glossary words, in the concrete form the reader would see ([Elements of Style, rule 12](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt),
   [Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)).
7. Each claim is a definite assertion that a linked page backs; no superlative, no exaggeration, no claim about what
   something is not where a claim about what the reader gets will do ([Elements of Style, rule 11](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt),
   [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/),
   [philosophy](../philosophy.md#what-they-get)).
8. One image, the lab, with one translation per term, used where it clarifies and nowhere else; no figure of speech
   the reader has seen in print before ([Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/),
   [philosophy](../philosophy.md#in-the-labs-words)).
9. Each paragraph carries one idea and opens with it; each sentence carries one thought, in short words, active voice,
   with its emphatic words at the end ([How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/),
   [Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/),
   [Elements of Style, rules 10, 13, 18](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt)).
10. The page is half the length a first draft reaches, and a kicker, slogan or box that restates the sentence beside
    it is cut ([How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/),
    [Politics and the English Language, rule iii](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)).
11. The page ends on the change for the reader, with one next step; it does not end on a list of links
    ([Elements of Style, rule 18](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134.txt),
    [Inverted Pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)).
